Small AI

Project Intro
Small AI is part of Slow AI project by AIxDESIGN. The Slow AI project seeks to imagine
alternative narratives for AI, unpacking and moving beyond Silicon Valley ideologies. Inspired by
this article about Timnit Gebru, and countermovements like slow fashion and slow food, it aims
to subvert corporate-first thinking by collectively exploring new perspectives—such as Small AI,
Ancestral AI, and Esoteric AI—as pathways to ‘imagine and craft the worlds we cannot live
without, just as we dismantle the ones we cannot live within’ (Ruha Benjamin).
Throughout 2024, we invited critical AI researchers, designers, creative technologists, and
artists to interrogate and share their ideas through community sessions, webwoven essays,
playful critiques, Miro boards, and zines—one hot compost pile that we hope sparks fresh ways
of thinking and talking about AI.

What could we make with AI if scale wasn’t an objective?

Introduction
AI has a killer brand.
We're sold a sleek narrative about AI. It's supposedly seamless, nimble, and capable of superhuman thought. The reality? We're nowhere close to this vision. AGI remains a distant dream.
What can today's AI actually do well? It excels at pattern recognition in structured data. Medical imaging analysis spots tumors with impressive accuracy. Language models generate good-enough translations between languages. Recommendation systems predict our preferences across streaming platforms. These narrow, specialized tools solve specific problems effectively. But, they operate within strict boundaries, lacking the flexible intelligence we've been promised.

Instead of pouring trillions into speculative futures that may never materialize, we should double down on what AI already does well to address real-life problems people face today. We need AI that serves humanity now, not a hypothetical superintelligence that may or may not ever be real.
Yet, investors continue funding the AI hype cycle. The gap between marketing promises and technical reality grows wider. We celebrate potential while ignoring the limitations behind AI's polished image.

This fall, at a conference in Barcelona, I saw an attendee set aside a chair for the AI in the room. This enraged me because the idea that AI takes as much space as a person is pervasive and perfidious.
If we set aside a chair for AI, then we would also need to make space for the people training datasets, those cooling the servers, and maybe even for the folks whose freshwater goes into a computing system just so that you can write a quote-unquote professional email or yet another report, written a thousand times over. 

AI is not a singular intelligent entity; it’s an ecosystem of people, machines, and places. Much of my work has been on unveiling this reality: The human hands and the very real, very finite natural resources behind AI systems.

In the Slow AI research project, I specifically looked to delineate a vision for Small AI that prioritizes people and the planet over profit. Inspired by Anna Tsing’s concept of friction (https://uk.bookshop.org/a/15206/9780691263519), I looked at the spaces of friction within AI systems, where its seamlessness slows down and stumbles. 

I zoomed in on the local and the small to explore how AI systems are not smooth but rather irregular and complex, with more details emerging the closer you look at them—much like the coastline paradox.

With the words of Michael Running Wolf,    founder of Indigenous in AI, in my mind, ‘If you go into a community with a mindset that data is just a resource, a monetary valuable thing, you’re fundamentally harming the community, and you’re also diminishing the value of this data’

I asked:
    • What makes small-scale data solutions work? How does placefulness shape these dynamics?
    • How can cooperative ecologies and economies ground Small AI, offering alternatives to profit-driven models?
    • What could community or collective data and AI governance look like? How do existing local initiatives challenge dominant narratives around AI?
I outlined an alternative to the bigger-is-better approaches that dominate AI development today. A vision that explores what AI could be if we didn’t design for scale or profit. Instead, Small AI calls for a more grounded approach, where AI systems are designed specifically for the human and non-human ecologies that sustain it.

Definitions

Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) 
A hypothetical type of AI that could match or surpass human cognitive capabilities across a wide range of cognitive tasks. Although presently theoretical, AGI envisions AI that can navigate real-world complexities like humans rather than excelling only at specific tasks.

Recommendation System
Algorithm that suggests or recommends additional products or services to users, based on data like past purchases, search history, or demographic information.

Dataset
A structured collection of data organized and stored together for analysis or processing. A dataset can be used for many purposes, including training and testing machine learning models, data visualization, research or statistical analysis. Datasets can be shared publicly or privately.

friction
The tension that occurs when different forces meet or oppose each other. In global systems, this refers to the challenges that arise when universal approaches encounter local cultures, bureaucracies, and ecologies.

New Frontier 
One AI story often told today is that we're heading into a “new frontier.” This narrative is rooted in extractivism and universalism – logics that can be traced directly back to colonial times.

In 1620, Sir Francis Bacon published Novum Organum, declaring that nature existed solely for human convenience (what Heidegger calls “enframing”). This ethos found visual expression in the landscape paintings of the time: wide-open frontiers of boundless land, empty of communities, ecosystems, and ecologies, waiting to be explored, exploited, and made into man’s image and desire. 

But the notion of the frontier was a lie. This narrative was, and remains, a colonial strategy—a justification for exploiting, erasuring, pillaging, and violently remaking the world. In the late Ottoman era, British rule, and the early Israeli state, settlers and rulers managed animal and agricultural production to assert control over both land and people, presenting it as an effort to “salvage the land.” (https://uk.bookshop.org/a/15206/9780262039079) 

In North America, colonial settlers adopted this ethos through the idea of Manifest Destiny—a belief that justified the westward expansion of settlers, the destruction of anything and anyone in their path. It was their Destiny to remake the land to, in Sir Francis Bacon’s words, their convenience, erasing Indigenous cultures along the way. 

The idea of the Earth as a vast, empty frontier, ready to be divided and claimed, has seen many iterations since—and continues to shape how we talk about innovation. AI is no exception. 

Much like colonial powers framed the "New World" as a frontier to conquer, AI is portrayed as the next technological frontier to exploit. Take note when you see people using “frontier” language to refer to foundational AI models.

In pursuit of our neo-manifest destiny, we plunder vast amounts of data, energy, water, and material resources for AI development. This resource-intensive approach ignores the toll on the planet and marginalized communities, especially in the Global South where most extraction and labor occur.

Just as colonial landscape paintings present land as empty spaces, ripe for conquest, mainstream AI narratives abstract and erase the social, cultural, and environmental contexts in which data is collected and systems are deployed. This frontier language sustains an unrelenting pursuit of growth and expansion with little regard for ethical, social, or environmental consequences.

The Eurocentric narrative of man versus nature—conquering the frontier, mastering the elements, and remaking the world—was never sustainable. It is at its breaking point. What wasn't viable then is even less so now. AI becomes a territory to be conquered—one where companies and nations race to claim dominance, paying little attention to the harms caused by this unrestrained ambition. With AI, we treat every behavior, action, and object as a potential data point—a resource ready to be captured, claimed, and exploited. We dismiss people and communities in pursuit of more and more data—or is it "AI fuel" —while funneling very real and finite resources like energy and water
into this one goal.

“Anyone who believes exponential growth can go on forever in a finite world is either a madman
or an economist.” Kenneth E. Boulding (1910-1993), American Economist


Definitions
Extractivism
A system of practices and mindsets that justify exploiting people, resources, and ecosystems through domination, violence, depletion, and non-reciprocity.

Universalism
The belief that certain values, moralities, theories, or treatments can be universally applied to all groups, regardless of culture, race, nationality, gender, and other social identities.

Generative AI 
AI systems that can create ‘new’ content - text, images, sound, or other media - based on patterns learned from their training data.

model collapse
A phenomenon where AI systems gradually degrade in quality when training on AI-generated content. As generative AI pollutes the internet with synthetic material—now estimated by AWS researchers to account for 57% of online content—this data is scraped and used as training data, leading to worse results and compromising the effectiveness of future models.

In the Cloud
Another story that obscures the material realities of AI is the so-called cloud. Big tech tells us our phones, laptops, watches are smart, wireless objects—symbols of progress untethered from the Earth. In truth, these technologies are bound by and to physical infrastructure, as authors like Kate Crawford, James Bridle (https://uk.bookshop.org/a/15206/9780141994260)    and Nicole Starosielski have shown. Our phones and laptops rely on rare minerals extracted from the Earth and are connected through an extensive network of cables crisscrossing the globe. 

These cables, hidden away from public view, reveal the limits of wireless technology and ground the cloud in a physical, material reality. These cables do not just cross oceans; they retrace colonial routes. Early telegraph networks were mapped over old shipping routes, and many contemporary fiber optic cables still follow these same paths. These routes then reflect the enduring legacies of colonial domination, where territorial politics continue to dictate the flow of information and communication today.

As Starosielski highlights in The Undersea Network, 99% of all phone calls, emails, websites, and even television signals travel not via satellites but through fiber optic cables laid under the ocean.

The cloud isn’t a cloud at all.
It's an underground network of people, resources, cables, and wires tirelessly working to make work like magic. Our collective ignorance about this physical infrastructure is no accident. It is shaped by a deliberate strategy of ‘security through obscurity’ —the idea that the public cannot disrupt or contest what they do not know exists. Much like landscape paintings framed territories as untouched and uninhabited, the cloud narrative erases the resource extraction, labor, and politics of territorial control involved in building and maintaining digital technologies.

Moreover, AI’s physical infrastructure continues to require our limited natural resources to sustain itself. 
Miranda Gabott’s research on the construction of new data centers in Catalunya—one of Spain’s driest regions—reveals this clearly. In 2023, Barcelona, the capital of Catalunya, faced one of its most severe droughts. This led authorities to limit each of its 6 million residents to 200 liters of water per day. Yet, during this same period, plans moved forward for building new data centers on the outskirts of the city.

Now, as we’ve seen already, data centers are infamous for their enormous water consumption to cool down servers. This raises troubling questions about the double standards between citizens and corporations during a climate emergency. 

Why are people asked to limit their basic water usage while water-intensive AI projects proceed unchecked?

The cloud is a narrative device, a symbol, or maybe a dream.    Let’s change it.

In the Cloud
The branding of AGI reveals still another myth shaping AI development: the idea that intelligence can and should be universal, detached from specific contexts and communities. Yet, when we examine how knowledge functions in various societies, we see that intelligence is rarely about universal capability, but rather about deep understanding of specific domains and contexts.

For example, indigenous communities have a deep understanding of local ecosystems, using controlled burns to prevent wildfires—knowledge often ignored in mainstream environmental management.

Or, translators working with endangered or poorly documented languages often know how specific idioms or phrases carry cultural meanings that AI won’t preserve.

This isn’t first time that dominant powers attempt to standardize and universalize knowledge systems. In India, the British Empire systematically redesigned the education system to support the colonial machine: the 1835 Education Act implemented a uniform curriculum that legitimized British historical narratives, imposed English as the language of instruction and official communication, and prioritized Western scientific methods over other ways of knowing.

In discussions leading up to the Act, Thomas Babington Macaulay professed his infamous Minute on Indian Education, where he dismissed native culture and learning as inferior, and argued for creating a class of Indians ‘who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern—a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect.’

The American Smile
In the article "AI and the American Smile", author Jenka Gurfinkel exposes how image generators and broader AI systems unconsciously encode and perpetuate American cultural norms, revealing the invisible Western bias embedded in these supposed "global" or “universal” technologies.

When reddit user @_m0us316_ prompted Midjourney to generate group selfies of people from different historic eras, it generated images that photo with a peculiar quirk – wide, toothy, and distinctly American grins.

This cultural mismatch highlights a fundamental issue in mainstream AI development. These systems are trained on whatever data is most accessible online – namely Western and, more specifically, American datasets. Generated outcomes are optimised to produce the most statistically common results, without understanding cultural contexts or nuances. 

As a result, specific cultural expressions—like the "American smile"—become encoded as the default "normal" standard, while others are sidelined.

And when this standardisation happens under the misleading banner of "universality," it becomes a form of technological colonisation that re-enforces Western norms while silently erases the norms of others.

It turned complex territorial and social landscapes into standardized, governable spaces, replacing local spatial knowledge with abstract European coordinate systems. Simply put, it enforced a Eurocentric geographical view of the world.

Universalism determines which problems are seen as worth solving, whose knowledge is considered valid, and who gets to participate in shaping technology.

This isn't about different forms of intelligence; it’s about understanding that subjectivity and specificity are integral to human culture—not outliers to normalize. Instead of striving for a single, one-size-fits-all system that tries to be everything to everyone, we can develop technologies that embrace subjectivity and placefulness. Placefulness
Placefulness offers an alternative to the frontier narrative of mainstream AI—that globalized vision disconnected from the realities of land and peoples, we discussed at the beginning of this chapter. Grounded firmly in place, this counter-narrative urges us to confront the histories of displacement, colonization, and environmental degradation. 

It draws inspiration from Jenny Odell’s call for sensitivity to the historical and ecological aspects of place in her book How to Do Nothing: Resisting the Attention Economy, amid continued digitalization and globalization: 

‘Against the placelessness of an optimised life spent online, I want to argue for a new “placefulness” that yields sensitivity and responsibility to the historical (what happened here) and the ecological (who and what lives, or lived, here).’
How to Do Nothing: Resisting the Attention Economy, Odell, 2019

Asking these questions disrupts the illusion of a void and universal landscape, demanding a deeper engagement with the layers of culture, history, migration, and cohabitation that shape every place. 
This perspective is not abstract. Living in Europe, it’s easy to overlook how migration and exchange have shaped everything around us. For example, in Spain staples like tomatoes and potatoes—so integral to Spanish cuisine—are not native but arrived through centuries of global exchange. Potatoes originated in Peru, and tomatoes from Mesoamerica, yet they are central to what is considered “Spanish.” Embracing placefulness means sitting with these histories and recognizing that culture and identity are layered accumulations of migration, exchange, and adaptation.

When it comes to AI, an awareness of place can help us think about AI differently in two key ways:
ONE.
It serves as a reminder that intelligence isn’t universal or placeless. 
Intelligence is contextual, specific, and subjective.

For example, when communities build and train their own systems for their specific contexts, they often achieve more meaningful and effective results than general-purpose systems.
While large language models (LLMs) may use broad content filters that inadvertently silence marginalized voices—similar to shadow-banning on social media platforms—community-governed systems can create more nuanced approaches to content moderation strategies that reflect local values and needs. 
A model trained on and for a particular community's needs will likely serve that community better than a massive general-purpose system trained on internet-scale data. 
This isn't about the model being “smaller” or less capable—it's about being more relevant and attuned to real people’s local needs.

TWO
Placefulness allows us to move beyond the reductive maps that typically depict AI infrastructure and, instead, envision maps that are more just, sustainable, and life-affirming.  Maps depicting AI infrastructure tend to portray entire regions as mere sources of raw materials, stripped of their cultural, historical, and ecological contexts. Minerals, supply chains, and data centers appear as isolated points on sterile schematics, with limited color palettes that erase vibrant material realities. 

An awareness of place can help us foreground the stories, struggles, and migrations that define the places where AI is built.
When we see our cultures, technologies, and systems as shaped by continuous movement and exchange, we open ourselves to designing with care and responsibility. Placefulness is about embracing the interconnectedness of our histories and acknowledging that we already know how to incorporate the unfamiliar into our lives.

Project D33pthroat
Mainstream AI systems – like ChatGPT and Claude – are not very usable for people looking for reliable, nuanced information about sex work. LLMs routinely:
    • censor “risk-adjacent” content, 
    • flag legitimate educational queries as platforms violations, and 
    • perpetuate harmful stereotypes that conflate consensual sex work with human trafficking. 
This technological censorship mirrors and reinforces broader societal stigma, making it nearly impossible for journalists, healthcare workers, and legal advocates to access peer-reviewed research or factual resources that could inform policy, healthcare, or advocacy. 
Project d33pthr0at emerged from this frustration.

Artists Gabriella Garcia and Arnab Chakravarty recognized how sex workers are forced to correct misinformation and educate others about their profession over and over and over again —an exhausting form of unpaid emotional labor that a Small AI agent could potentially address.
As with any emergent research, the team faced challenges that changed the project’s trajectory:
    • What happens when community consent doesn’t go how you expected it to?
    • What happens when your Small AI makes dangerous hallucinations? Even with a vetted data corpus, the Small AI agent still generated outputs that included harmful misinterpretations that could negatively impact an already vulnerable person.

'It might be janky, but its yours.'

Arnab Chakravarty

What emerged was something different than the original concept but perhaps equally as valuable —documentation of their process-oriented approach with a practical toolkit for people to conduct their own research into the matter.
Instead of launching single Small AI agent, Project D33pthr0at served as the first step (of perhaps many) to better understand how we might design, build, and deploy locally-run AI models that serves the needs of communities facing censorship or misrepresentation by Big Tech. 
This reflects a crucial insight: sometimes the most meaningful contribution isn't a finished product but a documented journey that makes both the ethical questions and technical possibilities of Small AI accessible to those who need it most.

The Pines of Kodaikanal

But how do we cope with, contest, or move within and beyond these layered legacies? How do we engage with landscapes shaped by histories of displacement, colonization, caste supremacy, wars, plagues, and migrations? Inspired by Richa Kaul Padte’s article The Power of ‘Placefulness’: The Pines of Kodaikanal, here I explore how placefulness offers a lens for reconciling with places imbued with complex and often violent pasts—and how this perspective can inform our use of technologies like AI.
Padte’s article reflects on her nostalgia for the pine forests of Kodaikanal in Kerala, India. These trees, now a hallmark of the landscape, are not native to the region. they were planted by the British during their colonial rule, part of imperial forestry projects designed to manage and exploit land for timber production. As we saw in New Frontier (page 11), this act sought to assert control over nature and reshape it into a vision of order aligned with colonial ambitions; a manifestation of power that sought to enforce imperial ideals of land management.

‘In other British colonies, foresting policies were not merely rehabilitation or beautification projects, as trees were planted for the sake of firewood and timber, used for construction, heating, and machine operation … The British considered rational land management and expansion an expression of order and control, it was also a reflection of their desire to transform the land and its creatures.’
Milk and Honey, Novak, 2023

Yet over time, these imposed landscapes take on new meanings, such as for Padte they give her nostalgia. The pine forests of Kodaikanal, though born of colonial exploitation, have since been lived with, grown into, and missed—as a distant childhood memory for Padte—despite what they represent.
I think that this paradox—of missing places shaped by violence—offers a powerful metaphor for how we might approach technology. Just as Padte reflects on growing with and around the pines, we too can find ways to work with technologies that may not have been designed with us in mind, or where we are not represented—ways to humanize, repurpose, or subvert technology that are true to our many lived experiences and realities. Moving beyond a strict dichotomy between the empire and the marginal allows for a deeper, more nuanced understanding. 

Instead of discarding places—both material and conceptual ones—shaped by colonial or capitalist ambitions, we can reclaim and reshape them in ways that address the climate crisis, honor local ecologies, and reflect diverse local cultures and ecologies. 

The pine forests of Kodaikanal remind us that places—and technologies—are layered with histories of transformation and adaptation. By embracing these layers, we can reimagine AI as a tool not of placeless optimization but of rootedness in the realities of the world around us.

Te Kura Hoahoa: Integrating tikanga Māori
Another place we find inspiration for what placefulness might look in the context of AI in the efforts led by Nan O’Sullivan at Te Kura Hoahoa: the School of Design Innovation at the Wellington Faculty of Architecture and Design Innovation (WFADI) in New Zealand.
Under her leadership, Te Kura Hoahoa moved away from Eurocentric education models toward a place-based pedagogy informed by Māori knowledge and values—a tradition that prioritizes the well-being of people and the place around them. Te reo Māori (Māori language) and tikanga Māori (Māori protocols and values) were integrated prominently throughout the curriculum. 
Crucially, this integration was not about reviving pre-colonial practices but about embracing Māori knowledge as a modern mode of design-making and knowledge-sharing, says O’Sullivan.

Central to this pedagogy are themes of kaitiakitanga (guardianship), manaakitanga (respect and reciprocity), and rangatiratanga (self-determination). These principles reflect a holistic view of design as an ethical and cultural practice, deeply tied to place and community. By incorporating these values, Te Kura Hoahoa’s curriculum challenges the extractive and exploitative tendencies of dominant design practices and invites students to engage with a more place-based and culturally resonant approach.

This integration has inspired projects like a LLM in Māori built by Te Hiku Media.  Te Hiku Media is a media organization that belongs to the Far North iwi of Ngāti Kuri, Te Aupouri, Ngai Takoto, Te Rārawa, and Ngāti Kahu. The project spans radio, online TV, and media, with a core focus on revitalizing the Māori language.
Te Hiku Media is also dedicated to archiving and training. Their LLM, built using just only 400 hours of spoken language from their archive, aims to share the Māori language across generations while developing technology that preserves its integrity. Te Hiku Media shows how technology can be developed for and with people, in ways that honor histories that came before them. 

Another example of kaitiakitanga, manaakitanga, and rangatiratanga in practice is the licensing of Māori resources and archives. The Katiakitanga license provides data access according to the tikanga (Māori customs and protocols). It is a “living” license that adapts to the community wants and needs. The license restricts open access and gives priority to the community to access and use data, acknowledging the histories of colonial extraction that privileged those who could benefit from open access. The Katiakitanga license allows us to prototype a framework for responsibility in AI governance that is about community well-being, the integrity of cultural heritage, and past and future generations. 

Placefulness invites us to design AI systems and governance frameworks that reflect values like kaitiakitanga, manaakitanga, and rangatiratanga. It suggests that the future of design and technology lies in remaking, reshaping, and reclaiming tools and practices to serve justice, sustainability, and local ecologies and customs. Placefulness reminds us that design is never void of bias or cultural positioning—it is always shaped by the cultures, histories, and values we choose to embed within it. 
Small AI is small because it is not about wholesale narratives—instead about moving into a future with plural of ways of designing and bringing AI into fruition.

Moving with Nature
Moving with nature offers an alternative to the mainstream narrative of AI existing in the cloud—floating above oceans and continents, detached from the material world. This counter-vision emphasizes the profound interconnectedness of all things, inviting us to ground AI design in the ecologies that make it possible and recognize it as tied to geography, resources, and the living systems it relies on.
Technology is often perceived as separate from the natural world, yet it is deeply "attached". 
By shifting our perspective, we can begin to see geography and geographical events not only as spaces for political strategy or economic competition but as the Earth itself speaking to us. 
Building data centers in drought-stricken regions or continuing extractive practices in fragile ecosystems demonstrates the profound disconnect the cloud narrative allows and perpetuates.

In this way, droughts, floods, and monsoons are urgent reminders of how we must build technology that aligns with our planetary constraints and rhythms. 

Technology designed with nature as a central consideration can break free from the extractive and destructive tendencies of big tech. It opens space for systems that respond to environmental realities, respect diverse ecologies, and prioritize sustainability and justice over profit and control. Moving with nature is a call to create systems that are not only innovative but also regenerative, antifragile.

The Mising Tribe
While reflecting on what it would mean to move with the Earth and its rising tides, I came across the work of Shweta Krishnan, a PhD candidate in Anthropology at George Washington University. Krishnan collaborates with the Mising Tribe in the island of Majuli in Assam (Northeast India), exploring subjects like human-nonhuman kinships and how these relationships inform ways of living and adapting to change.
In the High Theory podcast, Krishnan shares a story from her research. One of her interlocutors from the Mising community was worried about how she would understand erosion. Pointing at two villages separated by a river, the interlocutor described how, during the monsoon season, the river first swells and overflows and then recedes—always in unpredictable ways. Each year, this cycle alters the size of villages, shifting agricultural space and reshaping the land. Each year, the villagers adapt and move with the river’s rhythms, molding their lives around its fluctuations.

‘It’s not the river and the land … that causes the problems of displacement but the idea that human beings should remain settled in one place,’ 

the interlocutor emphasized. He went on to explain that the Mising community had always moved freely with the river, adapting to its cycles and shifting landscapes. The challenges of displacement then arise not from nature itself but from the imposition of fixed settlement—enforced through legal frameworks that restrict the movement. In Assam, these restrictions served to facilitate the optimization, and ultimately exploitation, of resources like tea and oil. Such legal constraints, and others like them, are legacies of colonialism and capitalism—systems that prioritize territorial control and stability over fluidity and adaptability. Erosion then is not inherently problematic, but the made problematic by the manmade systems that define lines around territories.

Krishnan uses the concept of deterritorialization, introduced by Deleuze and Guattari, to interpret this dynamic. She defines deterritorialization in the podcast as:
‘The axis along which formations get destabilized, along which a formation runs off and expands itself, pushes itself into new territories, and undergoes change. In the process, it produces complementary changes in all those other formations that it is continuous with.’
In other words, when one formation shifts, it produces complementary changes in all others it is interconnected or “continuous” with. For the Mising, the river’s shifts lead to corresponding adjustments in their land use, community structures, and ways of living. But so do the political shifts that impact their freedom of movement. The notion of continuity within the conceptual framework of deterriolization then extends to all things—whether ecological, social, or political—that co-exist, co-evolve, co-produce, or are in dialogue with one another. Even if that dialogue is not intended or not conscious.

In the context of AI, the story of the Mising Tribe shows how fluidity and movement can foster resilience in two ways:

Designing for unconventional and/or changing contexts. There are already interesting startups addressing this. For example, Ground News, a news aggregator platform ‘that makes it easy to compare news sources, read between the lines of media bias and break free from algorithms.’ It explicitly identifies headlines covered and/or ignored by “left” or “right” news media companies.   This could lead to new UX of AI. For example, in Nadia Piet’s project Feminist UX of AI, the Reset to Algo Checkpoints prototype suggests introducing checkpoints to allow users to course-correct machine learning. Or, the Switch Cultural Context offers users the option to select which cultural context best suits their needs.

Designing for the interconnected web of living and nonliving systems that underpin AI.  For example, the Website Carbon Calculator shows the relation between the web and carbon emissions through a “badge” that can be added to the footer of any website that automatically calculates and displays the carbon emissions of each page—aiming to increases awareness of web carbon emissions.  We can again look to Nadia Piet’s project Feminist UX of AI for inspiration. The Carbon Counter prototype envisions an UX of AI that keeps users informed about the carbon emissions generated by their use of generative AI tools.

Mini-festo: Hibernation is to die for
In the Summer of 2024, AIxDESIGN and Internet Teapot hosted the Small AI Zine-making Workshop. As a participatory extension of this research, the workshop used zine-making as a way to collectively explore and make sense of the Small AI themes.
Participants were provided with boxes of “ingredients” themed around nature, ecologies, and biomimicry, along with optional instructions to create a zine. 
My collaborator Leo Scarin and I received a prompt that invited us to select an animal with a unique ability, explore how it could inspire small AI technologies, and create a story about its potential use. 
We chose the wood frog, a remarkable and bizarre creature that hibernates by freezing to death and returning to life in the spring. This led us to consider the concept of season-centered technology—an AI inspired by natural hibernation. Based on cursory research, we identified key aspects of hibernation in the natural world, particularly for wood frogs:
    • Stocking up resources for sufficiency.
    • Preparing safe and secure spaces for rest.
    • Staying close to the community for protection.

From this, we created the “Mini-festo”: Hibernation is to Die For, imagining an AI that hibernates. This AI would follow a seasonal approach to technology, including:
    • plans for sufficiency that limit AI’s hunger for data and energy,
    • energy conservation,
    • periods of rest and data fasting, and
    • “death,” if necessary.
The more I reflected on this idea of moving with nature or seasonal rhythms, the more it resonated, especially given the environmental costs of year-round energy demands, such as heating and cooling, demanded by relentless production cycles. Seasons for rest, slowness, or even “death” are rarely attributed to AI systems, yet if these systems are fundamentally connected to the Earth, its resources, and people, why shouldn’t they be?

This notion challenges the relentless “always-on” model of AI, instead advocating for systems that align with the natural world’s rhythms. Explore the Solar Chat project to find out how internet beyond an always-on energy intensive paradigm might look like. It promotes sustainability, slowness, and thoughtful reflection. By shifting away from the expectation of relentless production, we could ask new questions:
    • How might we reduce AI’s environmental impact by modeling its activity after natural cycles, with periods of activity, rest, dormancy, and even death?
    • How might this foster deeper connections between technology and ecological systems to enable synergies and better coexistence?
    • Beyond efficiency, how might a seasonal approach to AI enable us to integrate cultural and philosophical perspectives into the way we interact with and develop technology?

Fruiting Bodies
While researching for Small AI, I found myself constantly at odds with the stories of infinite scale and general intelligence. 
Fruiting Bodies challenges that. It is a counter-narrative that shifts our focus away from a bigger-is-better mindset. 
Fruiting Bodies is about creating thoughtful, deliberate processes that support developing AI systems more connected to place and subjective experience. It encourages reflexive thinking to reimagine the possibilities of AI systems—not as instruments of industrialized general intelligence, but as tools that serve people and celebrate local wisdom.
This approach fundamentally shifts how we view data: not as raw material to extract, but as something organic to nurture—more like a garden than a mine. Seeing data this way naturally brings in care, consent, and thoughtful selection, transforming data collection from an exploitative process into a mindful practice.
This reimagining of our relationship with data is already taking shape in practical ways. In this section, I’ll share examples of applied AI that showcase the breadth of possibilities when scale in no longer the objective.

Data Intermediaries

Unlike traditional data brokers who primarily extract value from data, data intermediaries prioritize the interests of data subjects and communities. They often embed values like consent, fairness, and reciprocity into their operations, representing an important structural innovation that could help ensure more equitable and participatory data ecosystems.
Different organizations have emerged as data intermediaries—data trustees, unions, cooperatives, to name a few—creating new models for managing data. These show how inspiration for alternative data governance can emerge from unexpected places—from cultural institutions to labor organizations. What unites them is their commitment to exploring unconventional models that go beyond standard legal and financial protocols: they create working prototypes that reimagine how data can be managed for benefit of people rather than commercial profit.

Worker Info Exchange
Similarly, Worker Info Exchange (WIE) operates as a data union for gig workers, helping them access, control, and leverage their own data to improve working conditions and negotiate with platforms. By collectively organizing around data rights, they have successfully fought for (and won!) gig workers' rights in the UK and EU.
 
→ UK, 2021: WIE with ADCU helped drivers win a landmark case against Uber, where the Supreme Court ruled that drivers should be classified as workers entitled to minimum wage and holiday pay.
 
→EU, 2023: WIE successfully challenged Uber's automated decision-making systems, winning drivers the right to access personal data used in algorithmic management decisions. Defendant gig workers were awarded €584,000 in damages. 

These victories demonstrate how collective data rights can translate into concrete improvements for workers in algorithmic systems.

THE CALL, HOLLY HERNDON & MAT DRYHURST, SERPENTINE GALLERY, 2024
In March 2024, UK-based Serpentine Gallery put out an Open Call for a data trustee—a ‘trusted data intermediary’ who would work with over 300 singers across 15 UK choirs for months, testing new protocols to regulate AI training data through a real-world case study.
The experiment found three important insights:
    1 Data governance needs to go beyond simple yes/no consent because real trust requires ongoing relationships and clear pathways for people to share their preferences.
    2 We need legal frameworks for groups to exercise their rights together.
    3 Cultural institutions can serve as trusted intermediaries in AI development but they need the proper resources and expertise.    
Building on these insights, The Call helps create a data institution based on bottom-up, collective and participatory processes—an approach that allows for ‘the distribution of power between contributors to training data and those who use the models.’

If AI is a mirror, can we use it as such?
Working with object detection models, Vera collaborates with other artists to use AI as a mirror that reflects their subjective worldviews, encouraging creative self-reflection. In conversation with Linda Dounia Rebeiz

Linda Dounia Rebeiz’s work brings attention to the role of AI in cultural and ecological preservation, particularly in exploring what is missing or underrepresented in readily-available datasets. 
Inspired by Algorithmic Justice League’s research on visual recognition systems mislabeling Black bodies, she began questioning what remains invisible in AI training, who controls embedded narratives, and what gets included or excluded—a notoriously difficult task in the industry.

Once UPON A GARDEN

Her project Once Upon a Garden builds an archive of critically endangered and extinct flora from Senegal's Sahel region—a subject so under-documented she found almost no existing data. Using the information available to her (including family stories), Linda developed a generative AI model to visualise the undocumented flora her grandmother once saw. This four-year project not only highlights overlooked ecologies but critiques the lack of political will to maintain local archives—reflecting broader issues of representation in datasets. 
For Linda, training models with personal, localized data is an act of resistance against obsolescence and a step toward reclaiming underrepresented narratives. Through her work, she encourages people to appreciate “small data” and "small AI"—focused, context-specific tasks—as an alternative to the relentless push toward AGI. 

The conversations with Vera and Linda highlight how focusing on the idea of process in AI unearths insights with distinct, unique value compared to the output alone. Both artists emphasize the slow, iterative nature of their data work, underscoring that creating AI models is more than a technical endeavor—it is and can be a form of critical engagement. Together, they show that in the context of AI, the value lies not just in the final outputs but also in the process of training, refining, and reflecting. That, though, comes from two distinct questions: 
How can we identify what’s missing from the data?, and

What do these blind spots reveal about how we perceive the world?

Counter-mapping with Forensic Architecture
Separate, but related – Forensic Architecture, based at Goldsmiths, University of London, demonstrates how we might reorient opaque systems toward transparency and accountability by unearthing missing data. 
This multidisciplinary team employs investigative techniques that blend open data, architectural tools, and community knowledge to expose state violence and systemic injustice. Their approach embodies Audre Lorde's concept of "using the master's tools to dismantle the master's house," repurposing surveillance technologies and architectural analysis to confront power structures rather than reinforce them.

Their work challenges mapping as a colonial practice historically used to assert control. 
"In the history of colonization, cartography has always been in the hands of imperialists... Draw a map of a place to mark things and to exclude things."
Eyal Weizman, Founder Forensic Architecture

Through counter-mapping, Forensic Architecture subverts this power dynamic, creating a critical approach to cartography that amplifies marginalized voices and challenges traditional structures embedded in mapmaking.
Forensic Architecture illuminates deliberate omissions in data that obscure accountability, demonstrating how technologies designed for control can be reclaimed to expose injustice. By rethinking how we collect, curate, and contest data, they reveal that data practices are not merely technical exercises but deeply political acts with the power to reshape our understanding of ecological, social, and political landscapes. Their work suggests a path forward: repurposing the very tools used for oppression into instruments that establish truth and demand justice.

Triple-Chaser Tear Gas Investigation
Forensic Architecture, 2019
In their Triple-Chaser investigation, Forensic Architecture examined the global deployment of tear gas grenades manufactured by Safariland Group, whose CEO was a board member at the Whitney Museum. The team developed a machine learning classifier to identify Triple-Chaser grenades in images from conflict zones and protest sites worldwide. This investigation not only documented human rights violations across multiple countries but also highlighted the art world's complicity in state violence, leading to the resignation of the museum board member. The project brilliantly exemplifies using AI techniques to create accountability rather than surveillance.

Model ZOO
Forensic Architecture, 2018–Present
Perhaps most innovative is their Model Zoo project, which makes their custom machine learning tools available to other human rights investigators. This includes neural networks trained to detect specific weapons like tear gas canisters, recognize environmental damage patterns caused by different weapons, authenticate video evidence, and triangulate the location of gunshots. 
By open-sourcing these AI tools, Forensic Architecture has created a community of practice around using machine learning for accountability rather than surveillance.

Masakhane
In natural language processing (NLP), the dominance of English reinforces global tech inequities through missing data. Despite having millions of speakers, African languages like Igbo (27 million speakers) receive significantly less representation than Dutch Creole Afrikaans (17 million) or Icelandic (1 million). 
Masakhane addresses this imbalance by strengthening NLP research for African languages, challenging the notion that a small group of people within the few big tech companies – instead of the communities whose languages we are discussing – should decide which words, phrases, and nuances get “encoded” into our technological systems and which are left behind. 

The MasakhaNER: Know Our Names project illustrates how these imbalances affect everyday technology. Named entity recognition (NER) enables voice assistants and chatbots to identify people, organizations, and locations in text—yet African languages have been largely neglected in this development. When Siri or Alexa cannot recognize African names, places, or cultural entities, they fail African users entirely. MasakhaNER counters this exclusion by creating the first large-scale NER dataset for 20 indigenous African languages, developed collaboratively with local language coordinators.
Similarly, the Masakhane MT: Decolonise Science    project leverages machine learning to create scientific terminology and translate research papers into indigenous African languages, enabling knowledge creation in non-Western linguistic contexts. This collaborative approach demonstrates how Small AI can reclaim data to build technologies by and for communities themselves. As computer scientist Joseph Weizenbaum noted, ‘strengthening a particular technique by putting muscles on it contributes nothing to its validity.’ Masakhane proves that success shouldn't be measured by scale but by serving diverse communities on their own terms.

Another way is possible
The dominant image of AI infrastructure—massive data centers consuming megawatts of electricity and requiring industrial cooling systems—obscures the opportunities afforded by alternative approaches taking shape around the world. These examples showcase how AI hardware can be made and deployed at a community-scale, responsive to the local environment and available resources.

Potato Internet
The Potato Internet is a small-scale, eco-friendly social network powered by potatoes and built from recycled materials. Developed by Caroline Sinders in collaboration with Trammell Hudson and Motong Yang, the project challenges the idea that technological progress must always scale-up; instead, it proposes that localized, slow systems can be fertile grounds for creativity. By framing technology as something organic, Potato Internet opens up new possibilities for rethinking AI design with everyday objects that encourage us to reflect on the organic, local, and material realities. 
Potato Internet opens space for exploration, play, and reflection, enabling regenerative approaches to AI design. 

Sun Shines Bright
Computational Mama is successfully running her own LLM (TinyLlama and Gemma 3-1b) on a solar-powered Raspberry Pi, as it lies sunbathing in Udaipur.

The TinyML community enables machine learning to operate directly on small devices with limited computing capacity, offering a secure and energy-efficient alternative to cloud-based AI processing by drastically reducing data transmission needs and infrastructure costs. This approach keeps data processing at the edge, improving privacy and speed while significantly reducing energy consumption—a critical advantage as we approach 30 billion connected devices by 2025 amid growing climate and energy concerns.

In the words of Carolyn Hassan, CEO of Knowle West Media Centre, found via Careful Industries by Rachel Coldicutt:
“Community tech is technology that helps “value to stick to a place”
Small AI offers an alternative path forward—one that values situated knowledge and a process-oriented mindset over universalism, and longtermism. By prioritizing community value in both design and governance, it challenges the dominant narratives of AI that favor scale-over-everything.
The subsections—Placefulness, Moving with Nature, and Fruiting Bodies—invite you to see AI not as a disembodied force “in the cloud” but as deeply interconnected with land and the people who live there. Definitions
Reflexivity 
The ability to think about and reflect on your own thoughts, actions, or experiences. It's like observing yourself, your feelings, or your behaviors from the outside, and considering how they affect things around you.

DATA INTERMEDIARY
Like a data trustee, but instead of it being one person, an intermediary is an organization, institution, or entity that position themselves between data subjects (people whose data is collected) and data users (companies, researchers, or systems that utilize this data).

DATA Trustee 
A person or entity that serves as a fiduciary representative for individuals whose data is being collected, used, or processed. The data trustee acts in the best interests of the data subjects (the people whose data is involved) rather than in the interests of the organizations collecting or using the data.
Specifically, a data trustee:
    1 represents the collective interests and rights of data subjects,
    2 ensures data is used ethically and according to agreed-upon terms,
    3 advocates for data subjects in decisions about how their data is collected, stored, and used,
    4 provides oversight and accountability for data practices,
    5 helps navigate power imbalances between individuals and organizations collecting data, and
    6 can negotiate terms of data use on behalf of the collective.

Algorithmic Accountability
The principle that AI systems and their creators should be responsible and answerable for their decisions and impacts. This includes being transparent about how algorithms work and taking responsibility for their effects on communities and individuals.

Open Data
Includes data that is openly accessible and can be freely used, modified, and shared by anyone, including companies, citizens, the media, and consumers, for any purpose. 

Counter-mapping 
A practice that challenges traditional cartography by creating maps that center marginalized perspectives and experiences. It uses mapping techniques to resist dominant power structures and make visible what official maps often exclude.

Natural Language Processing (NLP)
A machine learning technology that gives computers abilities to interpret, manipulate and comprehend human language.  

Regenerative
The capacity to renew, restore, and revitalize its own sources of energy and materials, creating sustainable cycles of growth and regeneration. In nature, this refers to systems that can heal, reproduce, and maintain themselves, like a forest ecosystem that continuously rebuilds its soil and biodiversity.
